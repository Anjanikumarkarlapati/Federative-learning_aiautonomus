# Patient-level papers need pre-specified protocols and validation

A patient-level paper clears five separate gates, each set by a different body of rules: a reporting guideline matched to the study design (**CONSORT 2025** for randomised trials, **STROBE** and **RECORD** for observational work, **TRIPOD+AI** for prediction models), an ethics decision and a lawful basis settled before data are touched, a de-identification and governance chain that names the standard used (**HIPAA Safe Harbor or Expert Determination** in the US, **GDPR Article 9(2)(j) plus national law** in the EU), an analysis plan fixed in advance that includes **external validation and calibration** for any prediction model, and a submission package carrying registration where it applies, an AI-use disclosure, a data-availability statement and, increasingly, a patient and public involvement (PPI) statement. The guideline and ethics structure rests on official or near-official sources. The journal-specific rules do not: the research could not reach ICMJE, journal author guides or the primary HHS, ICO and GDPR texts, so those specifics are secondary and flagged below. Treat every journal rule in this report as a thing to re-check against the target journal's own author instructions.

## Matching the checklist to the design

The reporting guideline follows the study design, not the journal, and the 2024 to 2025 cycle replaced several older checklists. A 2026 randomised trial should cite **CONSORT 2025**, a 30-item checklist with a participant flow diagram that supersedes CONSORT 2010 ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11995449/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40228477/)). Its protocol counterpart, **SPIRIT 2025**, has 34 minimum items, a schedule-of-enrolment diagram, and new open-science and patient-involvement items ([Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03668-w)). SPIRIT 2025 is explicitly not a quality appraisal tool, so a badly designed trial can satisfy every item while still being weak ([Lancet via ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0140673625007706)). A prediction-model paper using regression or machine learning should cite **TRIPOD+AI** (BMJ, April 2024; 27 items plus a 13-item abstract checklist), which supersedes TRIPOD 2015 ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11025451/)). Its extensions are **TRIPOD-LLM** for large language models ([Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-024-03425-5)) and **TRIPOD-Code**, still at protocol stage, for code availability and computational reproducibility ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12888484/)).

The rest of the map is summarised below. The STROBE, CARE and CHEERS entries come from the notes and were not checked against primary text, so their item counts should be confirmed.

| Design | Governing guideline | Notes from the research |
|---|---|---|
| Randomised trial | CONSORT 2025 (30 items); SPIRIT 2025 for the protocol (34 items) | Supersedes 2010 and 2013 versions |
| Cohort, case-control, cross-sectional | STROBE (22 items); RECORD for routinely collected data | STROBE 2007 remains the base; not independently verified |
| Routinely collected drug data | RECORD-PE (15 items), used alongside STROBE or RECORD | Endorsed by ISPE; a 2023 to 2024 proposal argues journals should require it ([BMJ 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6234471/)) |
| Target-trial emulation | TARGET (21 items, 2025) | Must flag the study as an emulation and state the causal question ([EQUATOR](https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/22462/)) |
| Prediction model (regression or ML) | TRIPOD+AI (27 items) | Covers development, validation or both |
| Diagnostic accuracy | STARD 2015 (30 items) | Participant flow diagram expected ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4623764)) |
| AI-delivered trial | CONSORT-AI (14 added items); SPIRIT-AI (15 added items) | Covers AI intervention description, input and output handling, and human-AI interaction |
| Case report | CARE 2013 (13 items) | Includes patient perspective and informed consent |

Two further tools matter to reviewers even though they are not reporting guidelines. **PROBAST+AI** (BMJ 2025) is the updated risk-of-bias tool for regression and AI prediction models, with four domains and 16 signalling questions for development and 18 for evaluation ([Oxford record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:8b15c087-459b-43fa-ad03-1f66ed6ba370)). Reviewers will likely apply it to a prediction paper, so the authors should assess their own risk of bias with it before submission. Pre-registration plus a checklist is necessary but not sufficient evidence of quality, because reporting guidelines describe what must be stated rather than whether the study was sound.

## Ethics approval and consent turn on documented criteria

Ethics review and consent are the first gate, and the rules have tightened. The **Declaration of Helsinki**, adopted by the WMA on 19 October 2024, treats consent as an ongoing process and adds explicit protection for vulnerable groups, data protection, AI and fairness ([WMA](https://www.wma.net/news-post/revised-declaration-of-helsinki-adopted-by-the-global-medical-community-strengthening-ethical-standards-in-clinical-research-involving-humans/)). Sources disagree on how many revisions the text has had, and section numbers were not verified. In the US, the Common Rule allows a consent waiver under 45 CFR 46.116(f)(3) only when five conditions hold: the research poses no more than minimal risk, could not practicably be done without the waiver, could not practicably use identifiable data without the waiver, does not adversely affect rights and welfare, and provides pertinent information afterwards where appropriate. No waiver applies where a person has refused broad consent for secondary storage and use (secondary source; the regulation text was not fetched).

The legal distinction that trips up most papers is that **a consent waiver does not waive HIPAA**. Access to protected health information still needs an authorisation or a privacy-board waiver under 45 CFR 164.512(i). The research also found that the IRB, not the authors, should decide whether retrospective US data are exempt or waived, so a paper should cite the determination rather than self-declare it. In the UK, the ICO's research provisions under UK GDPR and the DPA 2018 usually make consent a poor lawful basis, but a common-law duty of confidentiality applies separately; the ICO's 2017 Royal Free finding (secondary) found research processing breached that duty. The notes did not check the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 or HRA and REC requirements, so UK-specific claims need that check.

For prospective interventional work the registration rule is firmer. ICMJE requires registration in a public registry at or before first enrolment as a condition for consideration, and accepts a WHO ICTRP primary registry with the 24-item minimum dataset or ClinicalTrials.gov. Ethics approval does not count as registration ([ICMJE](https://icmje.org/recommendations/browse/publishing-and-editorial-issues/clinical-trial-registration.html)). Observational studies generally fall outside the ICMJE trial definition, which is why a retrospective prediction paper usually needs a dated protocol rather than a registry ID.

## De-identification, governance and data quality are separate obligations

De-identification is the second gate, and it depends on jurisdiction. Under HIPAA, **Safe Harbor** requires removing 18 identifier categories and having no actual knowledge that the remainder could identify someone; date elements more specific than the year, tied to an event, must go, and ZIP codes are cut to three digits only under the Census population rule ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/de-identification/index.html); [CASRAI list](https://casrai.org/guides/18-hipaa-identifiers)). **Expert Determination** requires a qualified expert to document that re-identification risk is "very small" for the intended recipients, but the regulation does not define "very small" or the expert's qualifications. Exact admission dates or full ZIP codes therefore rule out Safe Harbor, and a limited data set with a data use agreement or an Expert Determination is the usual route. A DUA can supplement either method but cannot replace one.

Under GDPR, **pseudonymised data remain personal data** (Article 4(5)), while anonymised data fall outside the regulation (Recital 26). Health data are a special category under Article 9. Research typically needs an Article 6 lawful basis, an Article 9(2)(j) condition backed by EU or Member State law, and Article 89(1) safeguards such as data minimisation and pseudonymisation where it still meets the research aims ([Article 89 text, secondary](https://gdpr-text.com/uk/read/article-89)). Ethics approval does not supply a GDPR lawful basis. National research laws such as Germany's BDSG section 27 were not checked.

Governance is where the workflow gets stated in the paper. The UK model of trusted research environments follows the Goldacre Review (2022), which moved health data from direct transfer toward controlled remote access ([PHG Foundation](https://www.phgfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/The-federation-of-trusted-research-environments-for-genomics-and-health-.pdf); secondary citation). A typical sequence runs protocol and registration, then ethics or documented waiver, then data-access request to each custodian, then a DUA or site agreement, then de-identification against a named standard, then access inside a logged environment, then output checking before release. For multi-site and federated work, a 2025 scoping review of 39 articles identified 12 procedural, 10 relational and 12 structural governance mechanisms for federated learning, and a separate review found governance frameworks underdeveloped, with 16 of 38 studies discussing governance at all ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40640574/); [MDPI Healthcare 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/14/3/306)). Federation reduces data transfer but does not remove the need for a DUA, output checks, or disclosure risk from aggregate results such as small cell counts.

Data quality must be reported dimension by dimension rather than asserted. The most-cited framework for EHR secondary use is Kahn et al. (2016), which defines harmonised terms that the OHDSI Data Quality Dashboard operationalises as parameterised checks against OMOP CDM databases ([Kahn 2016](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Harmonized-Data-Quality-Assessment-Terminology-of-Kahn-Callahan/ae4bde838e928666f88a8f6dbb29c236227607a2); [OHDSI DQD](https://github.com/OHDSI/DataQualityDashboard)). The DQD check count is reported differently across sources (about 4,000 on the GitHub README, more than 3,500 on the OHDSI site), so cite the version used. FDA's drug RWE guidance (2024) frames reliability around **accuracy, completeness and traceability**, and asks sponsors to assess quality across capture, curation and transformation ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/171667/download)). Practically, each dimension needs the check, the threshold, the result per site, and the action taken on failure. Sites that fail thresholds should be documented rather than silently dropped. Timeliness, which the task brief named, is not confirmed as a standard dimension in the sources reviewed, so a paper that reports it should say the team added it.

Data sharing closes the governance loop, and the requirements are converging on "as open as possible, as closed as necessary." **Wellcome** requires data underpinning papers to be available at publication unless commercial, privacy, security or confidentiality concerns apply ([Wellcome](https://wellcome.org/research-funding/guidance/policies-grant-conditions/data-software-materials-management-and-sharing-policy)). **NIH's DMS policy**, effective 25 January 2023, requires a data management and sharing plan and states that consent is still required for human-subjects research even when data are de-identified ([NIH sourcebook](https://oir.nih.gov/sourcebook/intramural-program-oversight/intramural-data-sharing/2023-nih-data-management-sharing-policy); secondary summary). Synthetic data are not a privacy shortcut: synthetic records can reproduce real patients or be exposed to membership inference and linkage attacks, and no widely accepted evaluation standard exists yet ([arXiv 2501.09031](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09031)). Differentially private outputs need the mechanism, the epsilon value and how it was chosen reported. The notes' claim that k-anonymity is weak for patient data rests on background knowledge rather than a retrieved source, so it needs a citation before use.

## Prediction models need sample size, calibration and external validation

The statistical gate is where prediction papers most often fail. Sample size should come from the **Riley et al. criteria**, not the old events-per-variable rule of thumb, which the authors advise against. For continuous outcomes there are four criteria: a global shrinkage factor of at least 0.9, an R² gap of at most 0.05, residual standard deviation estimated within 10% of its true value, and a precisely estimated intercept. For binary and time-to-event outcomes there are three: shrinkage of at least 0.9, a Nagelkerke R² change of at most 0.05, and precise overall risk estimation. The required sample is the largest value across criteria. The worked example in the notes is 25 predictors needing at least 918 subjects ([Riley Part I](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/386405); [Riley Part II](https://utrecht-main-test.atmire.com/items/0ff8b358-9ca8-4b96-9b4f-247b8df46220)). The `pmsampsize` package implements the development calculation and `pmvalsampsize` the external-validation calculation ([pmsampsize](https://cran.nics.utk.edu/cran/web/packages/pmsampsize/refman/pmsampsize.html); [pmvalsampsize](https://cran.nics.utk.edu/cran/web/packages/pmvalsampsize/refman/pmvalsampsize.html)). The sample-size treatment of ML models, where effective degrees of freedom are unclear, was not resolved by the sources; the paper must justify its candidate parameter count.

Calibration needs more attention than discrimination alone. Van Calster et al. describe four levels from mean calibration to per-covariate-pattern calibration, and recommend **moderate calibration** via a flexible calibration curve as the practical target ([Van Calster 2016](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/577522)). Their 2019 paper warns that calibration curves need large validation samples and that model complexity must be balanced against sample size ([BMC Medicine 2019](https://bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12916-019-1466-7)). Decision curve analysis reports net benefit across threshold probabilities against treat-all and treat-none strategies, and it captures decision consequences that an accurate but poorly calibrated model may miss ([Vickers and Elkin tutorial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/6777022)). The debate over whether net-benefit differences need confidence intervals or significance tests is unresolved, so report them with caution.

The reason these requirements exist is documented by the COVID-19 prediction literature. Wynants et al. screened 2,696 titles and included 27 studies with 31 models in the original BMJ 2020 review; they judged reported performance probably optimistic, and found none recommended for practice ([BMJ 2020](https://bmj.com/content/369/bmj.m1328)). The living review later included 412 studies, of which 545 prognostic models were judged high risk of bias, though the counts differ across versions. The notes' reference to a late-2025 claim that only 28% of ML prediction papers met TRIPOD+AI is unsourced and should not be used. Missing data is the other recurring failure: multiple imputation is the default under missing-at-random, complete-case analysis is not adequate by default ([Sterne et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1136%2Fbmj.b2393)), and the paper should report missingness per variable, the assumed mechanism with justification, the imputation model, the number of imputations, and pooling rules. For causal questions framed as target-trial emulations, TARGET requires each causal estimand reported with its precision and sensitivity analyses for assumptions ([EQUATOR](https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/22462/)). Values for bootstrap resamples, calibration slope thresholds and standardised mean difference cut-offs were not confirmed by a source and need checking before use.

## Authorship, disclosure and submission need a complete package

The final gate is the submission package, and the authorship rules are the most stable part. ICMJE's four criteria remain the baseline: substantial contribution to design or data, drafting or critical review, approval of the final version, and accountability for accuracy and integrity. Contributors who meet only some of them belong in acknowledgements ([ICMJE PDF](https://www.icmje.org/icmje-recommendations.pdf); [CASRAI summary](https://www.casrai.org/guides/icmje-authorship-criteria)). A secondary source says the second criterion changed from "revising" to "reviewing" in 2023, and another says a January 2026 ICMJE edition moved AI into its own section and added a provision on access to underlying data. Neither was confirmed against ICMJE itself. CRediT offers a 14-role contributor statement, though its role definitions were not retrieved from the primary source.

AI disclosure is where journal policies conflict most. Every source agrees that AI cannot be an author and that its use must be disclosed, and that AI-generated images are generally barred. Placement differs: JAMA requires all AI use in Methods, Nature Medicine follows Springer Nature rules with disclosure in Methods, and NEJM and Lancet are reported inconsistently as placing disclosure in either Methods or Acknowledgements ([Manusights NEJM](https://manusights.com/blog/nejm-ai-policy); [Manusights Lancet](https://manusights.com/blog/lancet-ai-policy); [Manusights JAMA](https://manusights.com/blog/jama-ai-policy); [CASRAI comparison](https://casrai.org/news/ai-disclosure-comparing-nature-cell-nejm-jama-bmj)). The safe approach is to write the disclosure to satisfy the strictest placement on the target list, put any analytic or figure-generating use in Methods, and repeat it in the cover letter.

Other submission items follow the same pattern. PPI reporting uses **GRIPP2**, where the short form suits studies in which PPI is secondary to the research. BMJ reportedly requires a PPI statement in Methods for research articles and advises authors to consider GRIPP2; some journals only encourage it ([NIHR ARC NWC form](https://arc-nwc.nihr.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/ARC-NWC-GRIPP2-Form-and-Guidance_24.08.23-1.pdf); [BioMed Central](https://blogs.biomedcentral.com/on-medicine/2017/08/02/new-guidance-for-reporting-patient-and-public-involvement-in-research/)). The notes give the long form as 34 items, which conflicts with what the author of this report recalls; check the count against the official form before citing. Data availability for patient-level data typically takes the form of a restricted-access statement with a stated access route, and the journal's own wording was not retrieved. Preprints need care: medRxiv screens for ethics and trial registration, and arXiv tightened its endorsement and English-version rules between October 2025 and February 2026 ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/news/arxiv-2026-endorsement-english-ai-policy-changes)). Desk-rejection causes are inferred rather than measured: no checklist, no sample-size justification, no calibration or external validation, unexplained leakage or tuning on test data, and undisclosed AI use are the likely triggers.

## Conclusion

The paper's credibility is largely settled before the first analysis runs. A reviewer can check the protocol date, the guideline choice, the ethics determination, the de-identification standard and the sample-size calculation without reading a single result, and each of those is a precondition rather than a polish step. For prediction work the most underused step is external validation with calibration reported at the moderate level, and for federated or multi-site work the least settled is governance, where the literature is thin and the paper must describe its own mechanisms explicitly.

The evidence base has a clear gap. The notes could not retrieve the primary texts for CONSORT 2025, TRIPOD+AI item wording, the HHS identifier list, GDPR Article 89, ICMJE's current edition or any target journal's author instructions, so the checklist items, legal citations and journal rules above remain to be verified. The first step is to read TRIPOD+AI and tick each item, then the Riley papers and PROBAST+AI, then the target journal's own instructions, with the ICMJE 2026 edition and the NIH DMS template confirmed against their primary pages.
